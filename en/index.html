For individuals and business owners with more going on

Each decision makes sense. Together, they interact.

When you own property or a business, or have family and income on both sides of the Pacific, the tax is rarely hard because any one piece is hard. It’s hard because the pieces affect each other, and the advisors handling them often aren’t talking.

Your attorney drafts the documents. Your wealth manager runs the portfolio. Your bookkeeper keeps the books, and a tax accountant in Japan files the Japanese return. Each does their job well. But often no one is looking at how it all fits together, or in what order.

For example (an illustration, not a client story) You live in the U.S. and sell a family home in Japan that you inherited. The Japanese return is filed, the proceeds sit in a Japanese account, and the U.S. return waits until spring. Each step is reasonable, but the U.S. gain uses a different basis than Japan’s, and the foreign tax credit, FBAR and Form 8938 reporting, and the timing of moving the money all interact.

A business example (also an illustration) An owner picks the entity type with a bookkeeper, sets their own salary and distributions at tax time, leases a building they own personally to the company under a lease their attorney drafted, and talks through a future sale or handover to a child with their banker, each separately. Each decision is sensible on its own. But entity choice, owner pay, rent, and succession are connected for tax, and deciding them apart often means reworking them later.

Reasonable decisions, made separately, can add up to one avoidable problem.

I don’t replace your attorney or wealth manager. I make sure the tax side ties everyone’s work together, all year.

In practice, that means one point of contact for tax. When someone proposes a strategy, I ask what it does to everyone else’s work. When the rules change, or life does (a sale, an inheritance, a move, a business handover), I don’t wait for filing season to look at it.

This comes up most for people with assets, income, or family in both Japan and the U.S.; for rental property, where a sale or 1031 exchange has to be timed around the rest of your tax picture; and for business owners (entity choice, owner pay, how the business and personal returns fit, a U.S. business with Japan ties, or an exit or transfer planned alongside an attorney). Foreign-asset reporting such as FBAR is handled as part of that picture, not on its own.

Quick check

Do I need to file FBAR or Form 8938?

Are you a U.S. citizen, a green card holder, or a U.S. resident for tax purposes?

Did your non-U.S. financial accounts (e.g. in Japan), added together, go over $10,000 at any time during the year?

Bank and brokerage accounts, cash-value insurance, etc., converted to dollars. It’s the combined total, not each account.

How do you file your U.S. return?

Where do you live?

“Abroad” roughly means you lived outside the U.S. for the whole year, or were outside the U.S. at least 330 days in a 12-month period.

Total value of your foreign financial assets (in U.S. dollars)?

Foreign bank and brokerage accounts, plus foreign stock or funds held outside an account. Real estate you own directly abroad is not included.
